Private research memorandum · rev. B

Choosing a K–8 School in New Orleans

Three schools clear the bar. What separates them is a measure most published sources get wrong, and it says less than you would like about the grades River is entering.

The question

Three schools, one decision

River starts kindergarten. We are choosing where he spends the next nine years, and the schools worth choosing between are down to three.

I put every school in New Orleans through a pedagogy screen first: five things we want a school to do, listed below, applied before I looked at a single test score. Most of the city fell out there, including two schools with the best outcomes in New Orleans. Three cleared it: Audubon Charter, the International School of Louisiana, and Homer Plessy, where he is enrolled now. A fourth, The Willow School, clears it too but has the hardest admission in the city, so the odds of getting in are too long to plan around.

What we’re selecting for

  1. Whole books and sustained writing. Complex texts read entire, and writing long enough to require thinking.
  2. Real mathematics. Problem-based and discourse-driven rather than procedural drill.
  3. Arts as core, not enrichment. Taught daily by specialists, not a rotation.
  4. Screens as an occasional tool. Not an organizing principle of the day. A school marketing “AI readiness” or one-to-one devices is telling us something, and it isn’t good. The evidence for this is in Appendix C.
  5. Depth over credential. Work produced, rather than scores optimized and homework accumulated.

These describe the classical liberal arts, which is an old curriculum and not a new one. That’s the appeal: an education built on books, math, argument and the arts holds its value across a wide range of futures, so picking it doesn’t require guessing which future arrives. Section II argues each school against all five.

The comparison

The three schools across K–8

These cover the whole span River would spend at any of them, kindergarten through grade 8.

What you are looking at

Left panel, kindergarten through grade 3. Louisiana’s end-of-year literacy screener, which measures foundational reading: decoding, phonemic awareness, fluency. It is the only instrument that covers the grades River is about to enter. Three shapes: Audubon starts high and stays high, ISL starts at 22.8 and climbs hard, Plessy starts near average and falls to 48.5.

Right panel, grades 3 through 8. LEAP mastery in reading. Audubon and ISL both finish at or near the parish benchmark. Plessy never reaches it in any grade.

Why the panels don’t connect

They are different tests, and they describe different groups of children. The screener figures are all students at each school, the only cut published for K–3. The LEAP figures are River’s cohort. At Audubon and ISL the two almost agree at grade 3 (82.5 against 86; 68.3 against 68), so the join is close to honest. At Plessy they diverge by seventeen points, 48.5 against 66, because Plessy’s school-wide average sits far below the result it gets for children like River. Joining them into one line would produce a curve that matches neither measurement.

The kindergarten problem, and how much of it is already answered

LEAP starts at grade 3 and River starts at kindergarten, so the measure this report is built on says nothing about his first three years. The immersion finding disposes of most of that worry, but not all of it.

For ISL it is answered. The low early figures have a documented cause and a predicted end: teach in French or Spanish, test in English, and the penalty lifts once English instruction has been running. It does lift, on schedule. 68 at grade 3 becomes 87 at grade 4. There is no need to read 22.8 at kindergarten as evidence about teaching.

For Plessy it is not. Plessy teaches in English, from an English curriculum, with no penalty to invoke, and still falls ten points across the same four years, 58.6 to 48.5. That decline is the one number in the left panel with no explanation attached, and it belongs to the school he is enrolled in today.

What neither answers is that the K–3 figures are all-students and no cohort-level version is published. So the blind spot is a narrow one: we cannot see what Plessy’s decline looks like for a child like River, which is what the move turns on.

ISL’s shape needs one more note

The steep climb in the left panel comes from the language rather than the teaching. ISL runs kindergarten and first grade about 90% in French or Spanish, with English Language Arts appearing at sixty minutes a day from grade two. Genesee and Lindholm-Leary find immersion students match mainstream peers in non-language subjects, on the condition that you test them in the second language and allow for incomplete acquisition, which Louisiana does not.

But the right panel is not a smooth escape from that penalty either. ISL sits above the parish benchmark at grades 4 and 7 and below it at 3, 5, 6 and 8. Read the 3–8 aggregate rather than any single grade, and treat the jagged middle as noise in small cohort cells.

Section II

The schools

Three pass the pedagogy screen, including the school River already attends. A fourth passes but has the hardest admission in the city.

Cohort figures are LEAP % Advanced or Mastery, white cohort, grades 3–8, spring 2026. The citywide benchmark is 82 reading, 72 math.

Best fit · lottery only

Audubon Charter — Montessori

Uptown · K–8 · Free

81 reading67 math

Montessori runs on materials, stays screen-free by construction, and builds around long uninterrupted work periods and mixed-age classrooms. Literature is where it gives something up. Strong on independent research, weaker on a class reading a whole book together. Best combination of fit and outcomes on the list. No open-enrollment seats, and the window closes in two years since grades 1–2 are the only untested Montessori entry.

Available now · the recommendation

International School of Louisiana

St. Roch → Camp St. · K–8 · Free

80 reading70 math

ISL’s curriculum statement says every curricular decision gets tested against the immersion mission, and says outright that this isn’t the same as asking whether a resource is any good on its own. Art, music and PE sit in the enrichment block, which makes ISL the weakest fit on criterion three. In exchange: an oral, discussion-led method that fights the device trend by construction, and a kid who speaks a second language.

November is the only round that works

ISL requires no French or Spanish for kindergarten or first grade. From second grade up, applicants must already read, write and speak the language. The 2027–28 Main Round places River in first grade, so it is the last round he can enter through. There is no returning to ISL in third grade if Plessy’s numbers stay flat. Detail in the addendum.

Its low grade-3 figures are an artifact of the test. ISL teaches K–1 about 90% in French or Spanish and grades 2–5 about 80%, with English Language Arts appearing at sixty minutes a day from grade two. Louisiana tests in English. At grade 3 its cohort reads 68 against a citywide 81; by grade 4 it reads 87 against 80. Genesee and Lindholm-Leary find immersion students match mainstream peers in non-language subjects, on the condition that you test them in the second language and allow for incomplete acquisition, which Louisiana does not.

Read the aggregate, not a single grade

The per-grade figures oscillate rather than climb steadily. ISL is above the parish benchmark at grades 4 and 7 and below it at 3, 5, 6 and 8, so use the 3–8 aggregate rather than any single grade.

Incumbent · best curriculum, weakest delivery

Homer Plessy Community School

7th Ward · PreK4–8 · Free

67 reading49 math

The strongest curriculum of anything on this list, and the weakest delivery. Plessy publishes its materials, which almost nobody does. Wit & Wisdom is the most explicitly liberal-arts English curriculum in wide American use: whole books from classics to contemporary literature, fine art in every module as something to look at closely, top EdReports ratings, Louisiana Tier 1 for K–8. Illustrative Mathematics is problem-based and discourse-driven. Dance, music, theater and visual art are taught daily, with strings and band at Tremé. On all five criteria it is now the strongest candidate here, on confirmed evidence.

Two things cut against it. It hands out one-to-one devices from first grade, which I treated as the single criterion it failed. Answered · see addendum The school has since confirmed fifteen supplemental minutes a day, structured to free the teacher for small-group work. That is not a technology-forward classroom, and Plessy passes criterion four. And Wit & Wisdom’s own publisher says the program doesn’t systematically teach the youngest kids to decode, and recommends pairing it with a separate foundational-skills program. Plessy runs Fundations for that half, thirty-plus minutes daily. Answered · see addendum

Its math figure of 49 matches the Louisiana average for white students, against 72 for the same cohort across Orleans Parish. The open question is why a school teaching the right things produces these results, and it has got sharper rather than softer: with the device practice and the phonics program both confirmed, every curricular explanation is now closed. Whatever is wrong sits in implementation, staffing or history, none of which is visible from outside.

Best outcomes · hardest admission

The Willow School

Uptown · K–12 · Free

94 reading89 math

Arts-based, strong on the first three criteria, and the best school in the city for River’s cohort. Also the hardest admission in New Orleans. Worth ranking, but the odds are too long to plan around.

Excluded on pedagogy: Hynes Lakeview and Lake Forest

Both post excellent results and both fail the pedagogy screen.

Hynes describes student-centered learning using Louisiana Student Standards and Tier 1 materials, with an emphasis on engagement, goal setting and performance, plus Gifted & Talented and Project Lead The Way. Nothing in its published material mentions whole books, arts as core, or any restraint on devices.

Lake Forest is the sharper conflict. Its academics page calls it a college readiness focused school which believes it is never too early to prepare for college, states that homework reinforces the lessons taught during school hours, and requires holiday assignments to be completed upon return from school breaks, alongside a mandatory College Day Project, Duke TIP testing for seventh graders, and academic requirements for admission. Homework over Christmas break and credential optimization in elementary school put it last on criterion five, whatever its scores say.

The tension to hold

Outcome rank and pedagogy rank pull in opposite directions. Hynes and Lake Forest would buy River fifteen to twenty points and cost us the thing we’re doing this for. Audubon gets both. ISL does fine on both. Plessy has the best curriculum here and the weakest delivery. No test score picks between curriculum and outcomes; we have to decide which one we weight more heavily.

Reliability

Cohort cells run smaller than school totals, and Louisiana suppresses any cell below its reporting threshold. A single grade at Plessy’s size might hold twenty to thirty kids, so treat single-grade differences under ten points as noise and read the 3–8 aggregate. Audubon’s dip at grade 5 and Plessy’s spike at grade 6 are probably artifacts.

Section IV

What a move costs

No table settles this, and it belongs next to the numbers rather than underneath them.

Plessy teaches dance, music, theater and visual art every day. ISL puts art and music in an enrichment block. Moving him improves reading and math by a clear margin and cuts arts instruction by a real one.

Transferring now also pulls a five-year-old out three weeks into kindergarten and gives up the Plessy seat. If ISL goes badly, he re-enters through the same first-come process with whatever’s left.

Section V

What to do, and by when

Revised 24 August 2026

Three things came back after this section was written. Plessy’s device practice and its phonics program both checked out, and ISL turns out to have a hard first-grade entry rule. The recommendation below has changed: don’t rush a transfer before 4 September, and rank deliberately in November instead. Read the addendum.

The deadline · 4 September 2026

We can move River this school year without a lottery. NOLA-PS Open Enrollment runs through 4 September and places students into any school holding an open seat, first come first served. Audubon has no seats. ISL does.

Miss that date and the next opening is 1 October, which requires a documented hardship. Wanting a better school doesn’t qualify. After that the next clean shot is the NCAP Main Round on 2 November, which puts him in first grade in August 2027.

Call ISL this week about a kindergarten seat. Superseded · see addendum

Audubon fits better and ties on outcomes, but has nothing open. ISL is the only school that is both available now and clearly better than Plessy for River, by thirteen points in reading and twenty-one in math for his cohort. Starting in kindergarten also sidesteps the immersion penalty, since he’d learn the language alongside his class instead of a year behind it. Ask two things: is a kindergarten seat open for 2026–27, and what do I submit to claim it.

Call Willow the same day.

Best school in the city for his cohort and it passes the pedagogy screen. Also the hardest admission in New Orleans. If a seat is open now, take it. Otherwise it goes into the lottery ranking.

If nothing opens by 4 September, rank for 2027–28.

Audubon, Willow, ISL, Plessy. Use all twelve NCAP rankings; the algorithm only matches against schools you list, so there’s no cost to ranking deeply.

Plessy stays on the list rather than under it. It passes the pedagogy screen, staying carries no admissions risk and no disruption, and at least its weakness is measurable.

Appendix A

Method

Source workbooks

Every figure comes from four Louisiana Department of Education files: the 2026 LEAP Grade 3–8 Achievement Level Subgroup Summary (primary source for the cohort tables); the 2026 School LEAP Mastery Subgroup Summary (the spread table); the 2026 School LEAP Mastery Summary plus 2023 and 2024 releases (school-level trend); and the 2025–2026 EOY K–3 Literacy Screener Results.

Mastery figures combine Advanced and Mastery, which is the state’s proficiency bar. Cells reported as < 5 or > 95 keep those labels. Cells below the reporting threshold appear as n/r.

Why the white cohort

River is white and not economically disadvantaged. I computed both cuts. The white cohort makes the headline because more schools and grades report it, and the two agree on ranking at every school I checked. Neither substitutes for the other, since a school can post a strong white cohort and a weaker non-disadvantaged one if its white families skew lower income.

How I assigned the pedagogy ratings

I graded each school against published materials: curriculum lists where a school publishes one, academics and admissions pages, and stated instructional models. STRONG means the school says so and builds around it. MIXED means partial or implied. WEAK means the published material points elsewhere. UNKNOWN means no published position. These are arguments rather than measurements, and I source each one in the surrounding text.

Limitations

  • Private schools take no state assessment, so École Bilingue and the Episcopal schools rest on pedagogy alone.
  • Cohort cells are small, so treat single-grade differences under ten points as noise.
  • Grade 8 math is suppressed at most schools because students take Algebra I.
  • I inferred ISL’s device posture from its pedagogy. Plessy confirmed theirs.
  • Seat availability moves daily during Open Enrollment, so call rather than check the portal.

Appendix C

Evidence behind the screens criterion

Criterion four in Section III holds that screens should be an occasional tool rather than an organizing principle. This is the basis for it. It changes no figure in the report; it explains one of the five bars I measured every school against.

One-to-one devices are now standard. An EdWeek survey found 40% of districts gave every elementary student a device before the pandemic, and 84% did by March 2021. Common doesn’t mean it works. UNESCO’s 2023 GEM Report found the benefits vanish with heavy use or without a qualified teacher, and that across seventeen states only 11% of educators consulted peer-reviewed evidence before adopting an educational technology. A meta-analysis of 39 studies covering 1,812 children found lower comprehension for digital books when the medium was the only thing that changed. Most usefully, Warschauer found that once every kid has a device, what they do on it splits by class: lower-income students get routed toward drill, higher-income students toward research and analysis.

On a tour, ask how many minutes a day, which programs, and whether that time adds to teacher-led instruction or replaces it.
